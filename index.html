The banjo's story

The banjo was created in the Americas by enslaved Africans who held on to the music of home. With materials found in the Caribbean and North America, they built new instruments similar to the gourd lutes of West Africa, and kept their traditions alive through unimaginable hardship. The banjo became the heartbeat of early American folk and dance music.

That early musical landscape was actively cross-collaborative. Long before the modern folk revivals, and before commercial record labels divided music into segregated categories, dance and string-band traditions were shaped through direct, porch-to-porch exchanges.

My porch exists to trace that lineage with care and in community.

Roots · West Africa

Lutes of West Africa

Long before the banjo, West African musicians played skin-topped lutes such as the Jola akonting of Senegal, Gambia and Guinea-Bissau, and the ngoni of the Mande peoples of Mali, built on a gourd or carved wood body. A skin stretched over the body, strings of gut or plant fiber, and often a short drone string that the thumb keeps sounding. Researchers point to lutes like these as the banjo's ancestors.

Made from

  • Gourd
  • Carved wood
  • Animal skin
  • Gut
  • Plant fiber

Sources: Akonting, Ngoni

1687 · Jamaica

The strum strump

Visiting Jamaica in 1687, Hans Sloane described an instrument he called the strum strump, played by enslaved Africans. His drawing, published in 1707, shows a gourd body, a flat fingerboard and friction tuning pegs. It is one of the earliest records of the banjo in the Americas.

Made from

  • Gourd
  • Animal skin
  • Wood

Source: Afropop Worldwide

1770s · Suriname

The Creole Bania

Made by an enslaved maker in Suriname before 1777, the Creole Bania is the oldest surviving banjo. A calabash body, an animal skin held with wooden pins, a slim carved wooden neck, three long strings and one short one. It is now in the Netherlands, on display in an exhibit on slavery in the Dutch colonies.

Made from

  • Gourd
  • Animal skin
  • Wood

Source: Birthplace of Country Music

1785 to 1795 · South Carolina

The earliest banjo painting

The earliest known painting of a banjo, made on a South Carolina plantation, probably by slaveholder John Rose, and now known as The Old Plantation. It shows an enslaved musician playing a gourd banjo for a gathering of dancers. The gourd body and short thumb string match the African and Caribbean instruments that came before it.

Made from

  • Gourd
  • Animal skin
  • Wood

Source: The earliest banjo painting (Wikipedia)

1810 to 1841 · Haiti

The banza

In Haiti the instrument was the banza: a gourd covered in goat skin, with three strings made of pitre, a fiber from the agave plant. In 1841 abolitionist Victor Schoelcher collected one that survives today at the Musée de la Musique in Paris.

Made from

  • Gourd
  • Goat skin
  • Agave fiber
  • Wood

Source: Banjology, Duke University

1845 · Baltimore, Maryland

Wood replaces gourd

William Boucher Jr., a German-born drum maker who settled in Baltimore, became the first commercial banjo maker. He kept the skin head, fretless neck and short thumb string of the African American banjo, but swapped the gourd for a bentwood rim with drum-style screw brackets, so players could retighten a skin that sagged in humid weather.

In these same years, blackface minstrel shows carried this banjo across the country and overseas. White performers built a national entertainment industry on cruel caricatures of the very Black people who created the instrument, and those caricatures shaped how Black Americans were seen for generations.

Made from

  • Bentwood rim
  • Animal skin
  • Metal brackets
  • Gut

Sources: Smithsonian, The Met

Late 1800s · Parlors and factories

Frets arrive

By the late nineteenth century, frets became standard on factory banjos and the instrument moved into parlors across the United States and England. Fretless banjos never disappeared, and old-time players still choose them for their sliding, voice-like tone.

Made from

  • Wood rim
  • Calfskin
  • Gut
  • Metal frets

Source: Banjo, Wikipedia

Early 1900s · Records, radio and Ireland

Steel, segregated records and the Irish banjo

After the First World War, banjos moved from gut and fiber strings to steel, which cut through brass bands. Many gained a resonator, a wooden back that throws the sound forward, the setup most associated with bluegrass.

This is also when the music industry split the music by race. In the 1920s, record companies began selling Black artists as "race records" and white rural artists as "hillbilly" records, and radio followed the same lines. Sounds that had been shared on the same porches were sorted into separate genres, the start of the divide between blues, jazz and R&B on one side and country and bluegrass on the other. Even the instruments were policed: the Grand Ole Opry kept drums off its stage for years, until Bob Wills put his drummer in full view in 1944.

The four-string tenor banjo, loud enough to cut through dance hall bands, also crossed the Atlantic. In the 1920s, following American dance hall music, it entered Irish traditional music, moving from rhythm to melody and becoming a staple of céilí bands and pub sessions by the 1950s and 1960s. The Irish banjo carries the same West African roots, by way of the Americas.

Made from

  • Steel strings
  • Calfskin
  • Wood resonator

Sources: Banjo, Wikipedia, Britannica, American Songwriter, McNeela Music

1952 · The plastic head

Weather King

Calfskin heads loosened on wet days and could split on dry ones. In 1952 Remo began making Mylar banjo heads sold as Weather King, built to shrug off humidity. Most banjos today use a plastic head, while many old-time players still prefer skin.

Made from

  • Mylar
  • Steel strings
  • Wood rim

Source: Frets.com

2005 to now · Today

Every kind of banjo

Today it is all mixed together. Players pick up gourd banjos, wood and metal-rim open-backs, and resonator banjos. The Carolina Chocolate Drops grew out of the first Black Banjo Gathering in Boone, North Carolina, in April 2005. Since then Black musicians, luthiers and players of every background have helped bring the gourd banjo, fretless necks and gut strings back alongside modern instruments.

Made from

  • Gourd
  • Animal skin
  • Metal rim
  • Mylar
  • Gut
  • Steel strings

Source: Carolina Chocolate Drops

My path to the banjo

I was raised on Harriet Tubman Lane in Freetown, a part of Columbia, Maryland. My great-grandmother was a recycler. My family has a junkyard, and my grandfather ran a hauling business, bringing mulch to people and hauling away what they no longer wanted. It was a grounded upbringing, and it still anchors me.

We always had about ten cats around, and my grandfather raised me to have a holler to call them home.

I was raised singing alto in both the gospel choir at church and the school choir, from elementary school all the way through college. I dabbled with guitar and piano along the way.

Click to expand my timeline in musicCollapse my timeline
  1. 1990s · Columbia, Maryland

    Square dance and line dance

    In elementary school, a band came in with a washboard, a jaw harp and maybe even a jug, and the school hosted square dancing after school. I had never heard sounds like that before. They sounded wild and cool to me, and that is how roots music first caught my ear. I loved hearing it the way it was meant to be heard: with square dancing, in community. In middle school I joined the country line dancing team.

    I loved how the dance floor seemed to erase the lines of the school day. Bullies, popular kids, everyone else: once we were all out there dancing, that all went away.

  2. 2006 · Crete, Greece

    Tzouras

    Studying abroad, I started diving into folk instrumentation.

  3. 2007 · Film thesis · Guinea, West Africa

    Dunun

    As a film student, I spent my senior thesis filming a djembe orchestra: djembe, kora, dunun and more. Filming them started my own journey with the dunun set (dundunba, sangban and kenkeni).

  4. November 2015 to March 2016 · Shanghai

    Erhu

    I took erhu lessons in my free time while working abroad as a Disney Imagineer building Shanghai Disneyland.

  5. 2017 to 2019 · Los Angeles

    West African dance

    While I was learning the banjo's true history, I was dancing in Los Angeles and going to camps to deepen my craft, like Camp Fareta.

    With West African dance greats at Camp Fareta.

  6. 2019 · New Mexico

    The banjo's true history

    I had wanted to play the kora for a long time. Through practicing West African dance, I was searching for an art form that embraced both my African and American heritage. While looking for my music community, I learned the origins of the banjo, and I was so excited. Here was an instrument that carried both. It reframed everything I knew about American roots music.

    Gratitude to Brandi Waller-Pace and Amadou Kouyate for opening my eyes to the banjo's history.

    The dunun journey that started in Guinea kept going. Here I am on the full dunun set on my own.

The Natural World

Nature is where I find my joy, and I hold a master's degree in biomimicry, the practice of learning from how living things solve problems.

So many old-time songs are named for the living world: "The Cuckoo," "Little Birdie," "Shady Grove," "Cluck Old Hen." Wherever I can, I'll tie in the nature behind the music, from the birds and trees to the rivers and weather these tunes were sung about.
